No. 001

Does equality apply when talking about parental authority?

It’s difficult to bring out equality when talking about family. This is because each family is diverse and has their own way of living. In this essay I would like to explore the different views of equality we put upon family roles. I have amazing parents. This is undoubtedly true, but recently I had opportunities to talk with my fellow peers in class and realised that some parents don’t hold the qualities that I deemed normal throughout my short life. The point I’m trying to make is not that my parents are superior to others or that every parent should have qualities that I deem are good, but that parents differ from child to child. Of course, right? “Parent” is not the name of a new species. Before becoming a parent, individuals are just people, and the differences or quirks that come with them don't disappear just because they become a parent. So, naturally every parent is different in their own way, but we cannot limit the types of people that are able to potentially have parental authority. For example, you cannot say Bob can be a father but Mathew can’t. Something like that would not be acceptable therefore, we have to collectively hold the possibility to obtain parental authority as individuals in society. This seems to be the equal and right way. equality for the yet-to-be parents. How about equalities for the children soon to be born though? Surely that is ensured as well, right? That is the topic that has been bugging me for a while now. As previously mentioned, parents are diverse and every child belongs to a different type of parent. Yet, if we were going for absolute equality, every child should have the same parent don’t they? This is, of course, unrealistic. Furthermore, it will kill diversity between children which can’t be counted as a plus. Even so, the discussion of equality for children should not be stopped in society. To give our children a safe home to come back to, or to give our children someone to talk to. I and many people consider these things to be base rights that parents have the responsibility to give their children. That being said, when I talk with my peers in class, the words “lucky” and “unlucky” often come up when describing their parents. What’s more, this way of accepting your parents behavior based on luck seems to be the norm in today's society. This is the core of what has been bugging me.Treating how your parents take care of you as luck implies that some children that are not being taken care of under certain rights is a situation that is unavoidable. Again, the fact that yet-to-be parents are treated as equal in their opportunity to become one, yet the equality of soon-to-be-born children gets dismissed as luck. This seems hypocritical to me. What changes do I want then? I had an opportunity not long ago to talk to a person working at child protection service. He reminded me that what I’m feeling is logical and valid, but parents are just humans too. The possibility of a parent having several jobs to survive, or the possibility of them having a severe illness. These are only some of the variables that affect family, and I have been dismissing them while thinking about this subject. This was eye opening for me, but it simultaneously bugged me the same way the other subjects did. Parents have their own struggles as well. This is true, but why does that mean the child has to take the burden that comes from those circumstances. This train of thought finally concreted my opinion when talking about equality in family. That parental authority should be a licence not a right. Earlier in this essay, I explained how everyone should hold the possibility to have parental authority. Both Bob and Mathew should have a chance. By changing parental authority to a licence, it doesn’t take that chance away from anyone. Nonetheless, by making it a licence we as a society can standardize the yet-to-be parents to a certain extent. In other words, we can ensure that children would get the basic rights with no correlation to the parents circumstances. This is incorporating a fragment of making every child's parent the same, without destroying individuality. If Japan were to implement this system, it would further fuel the declining birthrate, so I doubt it will ever happen, but that is a topic for another time.
